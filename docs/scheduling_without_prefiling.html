What scheduling alone can do for a judge's day

If we drop the pre-filing check and every input from the parties, and keep only what the registry controls when it builds the cause list, how far does that get us? We tested it on the hackathon's own data.

The answer

Scheduling alone nearly doubles disposals. On the organisers' data, fitting the list to the minutes and setting real next dates takes 60-day disposals from 196 to 329, against 356 with everything on, and cuts wasted listings by 83%. Readiness information (whether summons and warrants are back, counsel's confirmation, the last hearing's note) raises how often a heard case moves forward: 65% with scheduling alone, 79% with everything. Over a year, pre-filing matters for new complaints: 91% get past cognizance, against 32% without it.

Why scheduling alone works

Three registry moves carry most of the gain

List only what fits

Today 57 cases are listed and the court reaches 44% of them. The rest wait all day and go home. Adding up each case's expected minutes and stopping at 95% of the day brings reach to 97%.

Spend minutes where hearings succeed

Each case is scored by its priority times its chance of moving forward, per minute. Types that rarely succeed today, such as warrant returns (13.5%) and reports (8.3%), stop crowding out evidence and judgments.

A real next date

The gap comes from the next purpose in the organisers' table (5 to 45 days), not a flat 60. Cases come back when there is something to hear.

The cost of dropping readiness

Without knowing whether a warrant is back, the scheduler can only judge each type by its average. All warrant-return cases wait longer, including the ones that were ready. Heard cases move forward 67% of the time rather than 79%, and wasted listings are 439 rather than 259.

The two optional boxes are what this test switched off. Everything from priority onwards is the registry's scheduling.

  1. Priority. Each case gets a score from its hearing type, how long it has waited and how ready it is. Bail and other liberty matters come first, always.
  2. Prediction. For each case: the chance both sides appear, the chance the hearing moves the case forward, and the expected minutes. Adjourning still costs about 2 minutes.
  3. Which day. CP-SAT picks the best set of cases for each day across all three courts. A quarter of each day is locked for cases over 4 years old. Nothing is listed past 95% of the day's minutes.
  4. What time. CP-SAT sets exact start times. No advocate is due in two courtrooms at once, with 10 minutes to walk between them, and similar cases sit together.
  5. Human decision. The judge sees the list with a reason for each case, adds or removes cases and sees the cost first, then approves.
  6. Feedback. One tap records the outcome. The next date follows, and the models learn from what happened.

The cost of waiting grows over time and jumps at legal deadlines. Bail doubles at 60 days, when default bail becomes available.

The model

Measured rates, read per case

What is estimatedHow
Will this hearing move the case?The organisers' measured rate for its hearing type (plea 90%, warrant 14%), multiplied 2.5 times when the last order signals a risk ("Await warrant", "Absent: Accused", "not ready"), with each type's average held at the measured rate
If not, why?The real mix of failure reasons for that type: process not back (persists until the return), a party absent, not ready, court, unclear
How long will it take?The organisers' estimated minutes per type, with spread; an adjournment still costs 2 minutes
How does each lever change it?Stated assumptions in config: process status known 90%, confirmation removes half of "not ready", fixed slots remove a third of absences. Each is switched off in turn to show its worth

Today's rules on this model come close to the handbook's day without any tuning: 57 listed, 25 reached, 12 moving the case, against the handbook's 60, 20 and 10.

Pre-filing

The registry's scrutiny, done at e-filing

What is checked

For a cheque-dishonour complaint: cheque presented within 3 months, demand notice within 30 days of the return memo, 15 days for the drawer to pay, complaint within a month after that; the documents; the accused's address, phone and email so summons can go electronically; and jurisdiction, with the s.225 enquiry flagged when the accused lives outside the court's area. Nothing is refused: the result says what to cure.

What it changes

In the organisers' data, 96 of 100 cases went through delay-condonation hearings (3.4 each, 29% moving the case). Computing limitation at filing and hearing condonation with admission removes that track for new complaints, and complete summons details attack the largest failure pool: a summons or warrant not back, behind 196 of 293 failed warrant hearings.

The practical plan: ship scheduling first, since it needs only the registry's own data and gives most of the gain. Then add readiness as the court's systems expose process status, and move the registry's scrutiny into the e-filing form.
